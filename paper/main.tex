\begin{abstract}
A market maker on an options book loses to counterparties who know something and earns from those who do
not, but on an anonymous venue the two can only be told apart by proxy. Derive settles on chain, so its
public tape names both wallets. On 621\,508 fills in BTC, ETH and HYPE options between January 2024 and
September 2026, the maker's mean markout thirty minutes after the fill is $+12.79$ USDC per contract: a half
spread of $+15.45$ plus adverse selection of $-2.66$. Fees, rebates and a modelled hedge cost leave a net
edge of $+9.34$. The loss is concentrated: ten taker wallets carry 90.1 per cent of it, four of them nearly
all fills of the two professional counterparty classes. Trade size, the standard proxy for toxic flow,
explains none of it under instrument-by-day fixed effects, and sweeps predict only the move of
the underlying, which hedging removes. Four hypotheses were registered before any markout was computed; three
are rejected.

\section{Introduction}
A market maker on an options book loses money to counterparties who know something and earns it from those
who do not. Telling the two apart is the central problem of the literature that follows
\citet{glosten1985} and \citet{copeland1983}. Because the counterparty is anonymous, toxicity has to be
inferred from what is observable about the trade itself, above all its size and its aggressiveness
\citep{easleyohara1987}. That inference is indirect, and it is the weakest link in any estimate of what an
options maker actually earns.
% src text:two_kinds two

Derive is an options venue whose book is matched off chain and settled on chain. Every fill lands in a
public tape that names both wallets, the subaccount, the request-for-quote identifier where there is one,
the fee, the rebate and the realised profit of each side. The counterparty is not a statistical construct
but an address with a history. This paper uses that to ask directly what the literature has had to approach
by proxy: against whom does an options maker lose? In equity options the question has been answered through
the price impact of net demand \citep{bollen2004,garleanu2009} and through the returns that follow order
flow \citep{muravyev2016}, always with the counterparty unobserved \citep{easley1998}.

The answer is that the maker's average result is a statement about the counterparty rather than about the
option. Taking the passive side of all 621\,508 fills in the sample, the mean markout after thirty minutes
is $+12.79$ USDC per contract, while the median fill returns $+0.96$. Splitting that average by counterparty
class moves it from $+24.46$ against ordinary takers to $-25.40$ against the seventeen wallets that dominate
maker volume. Nothing about the option changes across that range; only the address on the other side does.

The loss is carried by very few of them. Ten taker wallets account for 90.1 per cent of the maker's
aggregate loss, and a single address for 38.0 per cent. Four of these ten supply 98.8 per cent of the
fills of the dominant-maker class and 92.0 per cent of those of the market-maker programme (MM programme), and
carry 74.0 per cent of the loss on their own, so the concentration result and the counterparty result largely
coincide. What does not follow is the
usual explanation. Once instrument-by-day fixed effects absorb the composition of the flow, trade size above the
ninetieth percentile carries no coefficient distinguishable from zero, and sweeps across several strikes carry
one only in USDC, through the next move of the underlying, which a delta hedge removes. On this venue the standard observables do not find the informed trader; the identity does.

Three contributions follow. The first is a measurement. Section~\ref{sec:measure} decomposes the maker's
result into a half spread, adverse selection, the fee, the rebate and the cost of delta hedging, marked
against the volatility surface that the venue itself writes on chain rather than against a mark of my own
construction. The second is the counterparty fact above, together with its negative half: the observables
that the literature uses in place of identity do not reproduce it. The third, exploratory, is a map. Section~\ref{sec:maker}
reports the net edge per unit of notional across moneyness and tenor for three underlyings, which is the
form in which a quoting decision is actually taken.

\section{The venue and the tape}\label{sec:venue}
Crypto markets are fragmented across venues with very different designs \citep{makarov2020}. Derive matches
orders off chain and settles them, in the dollar stablecoin USDC, on an Optimism-stack chain of its own. Positions are held
in subaccounts under the venue's standard or portfolio margin, and the mark that margin is computed against is not a
private number. A feed contract receives, for every expiry, the parameters of a stochastic volatility
inspired (SVI) surface \citep{gatheral2014}, and anyone can read the mark implied volatility of any strike at any
block by evaluating that surface. During the sample the median curve was 32 seconds old at the moment of a
fill and the ninetieth percentile was 58 seconds.
% src text_numbers.json:curve_age_median_s 32
% src text_numbers.json:curve_age_p90_s 58

The public trade tape is unusual: it is not anonymous. Each fill appears twice, once from each side,
and carries the wallet and subaccount of both parties, the transaction hash, the fee and rebate booked to
each side, and the realised profit. Where a trade came from a request for quote (RFQ), the tape carries the
identifier that ties its legs together; 20.7 per cent of fills arrive this way.
% src text_numbers.json:rfq_share_pct 20.7

The sample runs from 11 January 2024 to 30 September 2026 and covers the option fills on BTC, ETH and HYPE,
the token of Hyperliquid, that pass the registered exclusions: 621\,508 fills across 43\,495 instruments, 12\,019 taker wallets and 7\,264 maker wallets. ETH carries
414\,707 fills, BTC 159\,467 and HYPE 47\,334. Liquidations do not appear: they are settled outside the
trade tape, so the liquidation class defined in the pre-registration is empty throughout.

Almost everything known about adverse selection in decentralised markets concerns automated market makers,
where the liquidity provider does not quote but posts a curve, and where the loss to arbitrageurs is a
mechanical consequence of that curve rather than a decision \citep{milionis2022,cartea2024,lehar2025}. A
central limit order book with a maker who chooses a price is a different object, closer to the venues
studied by \citet{hasbrouck1993} and \citet{menkveld2013} than to a constant-product pool, and the evidence
on decentralised market quality has so far been drawn from the pool side \citep{barbon2024,aramonte2021}.
Prices on this venue are quoted by people, and what follows measures what those quotes earned.

What the venue does not publish is the history of the book itself. Quotes that were never hit, the depth
standing at any moment, and cancellations are absent, so every statement in this paper is about trades that
happened and none is about the cost of quoting. Section~\ref{sec:limits} returns to this.

\section{What a markout measures}\label{sec:measure}
For every fill I take the maker's side, $s = -1$ when the taker bought and $s = +1$ when the taker sold, and
compare the traded price $P$ with the venue's mark $M$ at a series of horizons. The markout at horizon
$\tau$ is $\mathrm{MO}_\tau = s\,(M(t+\tau) - P)$. At $\tau = 0$ this is the half spread, the distance
between the fill and the mark at the moment of the fill, and the difference $\mathrm{MO}_\tau -
\mathrm{MO}_0$ is adverse selection: what the mark did afterwards. Figure~\ref{fig:t1} shows the
construction on a single fill. The two pieces sum to the markout, so the half spread is not added a second
time when the maker's result is totalled.

Three marks are available and they answer different questions. The primary one is the on-chain surface,
evaluated at the last curve pushed at or before the horizon, because the venue uses it

exact but available once: over the 597\,737 fills settled inside the loaded data its mean markout is
$+16.75$ USDC per contract (95 per cent interval $-3.94$ to $36.37$), and it enters the secondary test of H2.

Four units are used, and none is redundant. USDC per contract is the quantity the pre-registration
fixed. Vol points, implied volatility in percentage points, remove the move of the underlying and pool across underlyings whose contracts differ in size by
three orders of magnitude. The share of the premium is what a maker books. Basis points of notional is what a
quoting decision compares against a perpetual spread. Figure~\ref{fig:f1} shows why reading the result in
only one of them is misleading.

Counterparties are assigned to one class each by a cascade in which the first match wins: liquidation,
vault, request for quote, dominant maker, market-maker programme, large wallet, and otherwise ordinary
taker. A dominant maker is a wallet that supplied a large share of maker volume in the same month; the
programme membership comes from the venue's own published epochs. Figure~\ref{fig:f3} shows the cascade
with the fills it assigns.

\section{Pre-registration and inference}\label{sec:prereg}
Four hypotheses were committed, with their rejection rules, on 17 September 2026 before any markout existed.
Two dated addenda settled definitions before the first inference; a third, written after
the first version, corrects the unit of fee and rebate and changes no hypothesis, threshold or sample. H1 holds that toxic flow is concentrated in few wallets \emph{and} identifiable from trade size and
sweeps; it is rejected unless the upper bound of its 90 per cent interval reaches one half and both coefficients are
negative and significant. H2 holds that vault flow is uninformed; it is rejected if the mean markout against vaults is
significantly negative. H3 holds that HYPE flow on Derive was more toxic before HYPE options were listed on a
centralised venue on 23 June 2026 than after; it is rejected unless the difference-in-differences estimate is
positive, significant, and extreme against a placebo distribution. H4 holds that the net edge is positive in
fewer than half of the moneyness-by-tenor cells and negative in short-dated at-the-money BTC and ETH; it is
rejected if half of the cells, or either of those, carry a positive interval.
% src const:hypotheses Four
% src const:prereg_day 17 September 2026
% src const:definition_addenda Two
% src const:interval_pct 90
% src const:hype_event 23 June 2026

All inference clusters on the taker wallet, the level at which sampling is plausibly independent
\citep{abadie2023}. A coefficient meets a rule at $|t| \geq 1.96$ with the cluster-robust $t$, and its wild cluster bootstrap
$p$-value, with restricted residuals, Rademacher weights \citep{cameron2008,mackinnon2017}, $B = 9\,999$ draws
and a fixed seed \citep{roodman2019}, always agrees; means and cell averages carry percentile intervals from a cluster pairs bootstrap with
the same $B$. Fixed effects, applied as a within transformation, are instrument by day with delta-bucket dummies for H1
and day for H3. Error bars and bands drawn only in a figure use 999 draws or fewer.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The average is not a trading outcome}
The mean markout is thirteen times the median, and the gap is not a curiosity but the first thing a reader
has to absorb. Weighting each fill by the contracts it moved rather than counting it once takes the average
from $+12.79$ to $+0.266$ USDC per contract; trimming five per cent from each tail takes it to $+6.24$ (both exploratory).
Figure~\ref{fig:f1} shows why. The three underlyings contribute similar aggregate dollar amounts to
the maker, 3.66, 4.12 and 3.31 million USDC in BTC, ETH and HYPE, while their average index prices differ by a factor of
1\,520. A per-contract average is therefore in large part a statement about contract size. Expressed as a
share of the premium, both taken per contract, the distribution a maker books runs from $-3.3$ per cent at
the lower quartile through $+3.4$ per cent at the median to $+13.5$ per cent at the upper quartile (exploratory). Every number that
follows is reported in the unit that makes it comparable, and the pre-registered unit is kept alongside.

\section{What this means for a maker}\label{sec:maker}
A quoting decision is not taken in USDC per contract. It is taken per unit of risk, against the alternative
of quoting the same notional somewhere else. Measured that way, net of fees, rebate and hedge, Figure~\ref{fig:f5}
reads differently (exploratory). At the money the median rises from 2.8 basis points at two days or less to 5.6 at thirty
to ninety days in BTC, from 3.8 to 10.0 in ETH and from 5.7 to 24.4 in HYPE. BTC, which pays most per contract, pays least per notional: its median over the cells is 3.2
basis points, against 5.7 in ETH and 15.6 in HYPE. The first consequence is that the margin is thinnest
where the flow is. Options of up to seven days and an absolute delta below 60 per cent carry close to half
of the BTC and ETH fills and pay 1.0 to 2.8 and 1.6 to 3.8 basis points respectively. That leaves little
room for costs the model does not see: at a perpetual half spread of three basis points the share of
positive cells falls to 56.6 per cent.

The second consequence concerns what a maker should spend effort on. The result of Section~\ref{sec:results}
is that size and aggressiveness carry no information about the hedged outcome once the instrument and the day are
held fixed, while the identity of the counterparty carries almost all of it. A maker who prices flow by its
observable shape is modelling the wrong variable. A maker who maintains a list of a dozen addresses and
widens against them is using the variable that works, and on a venue that settles on chain that list is
cheap to build and needs no inference at all. The concentration makes the policy tractable: ten wallets, not
thousands.
% src const:top_wallets ten

The third is a warning about the map itself. It describes fills that happened at prices that cleared. It
does not describe what a new quote would have earned, because a quote that is never hit leaves no trace in
the data. A cell with a large positive edge and few fills may be a cell where the maker was rarely willing
to quote, not one where quoting was profitable. Sizing a strategy from this map without the book history
would mistake selection for opportunity.

\section{What this cannot show}\label{sec:limits}
Seven limits bound the reading of the results, and each is stated once. First, the mark is the venue's own
surface, so the maker is measured against the number the venue uses rather than against an independent
valuation; Figure~\ref{fig:a1} shows no trend in the mean markout across eight classes of curve age, from
$11.30$ to $16.83$ USDC per contract within wide intervals (exploratory), so the lag of the curve does not
drive the result; its panel c sets the forward of the curve against the index. Second, the alternative mark
taken from the next fill in the same instrument arrives at a median distance of 15\,011 seconds and is
therefore not a control at a thirty-minute horizon; where the comparison is fair, with a next fill inside
300 seconds, the two paths correlate at $0.898$ (exploratory). Third, one trader may hold several addresses, so
the concentration figures in this paper likely understate it. Fourth, the vault class rests on eight wallets,
so few that the bootstrap itself is fragile, which is why H2 is reported as a hint that vault flow is not
toxic and not as a demonstration. Fifth,
liquidations settle outside the trade tape, so the paper says nothing about the flow that a maker probably
fears most. Sixth, the volatility unit is undefined for 11\,059 fills whose traded price sits outside the
arbitrage bounds, and those fills are excluded from every vol-point statement. Seventh, and most
importantly, the venue publishes no history of the book, so nothing here measures the cost of quoting, only
the outcome of trading.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
On a venue where the counterparty is an address rather than an inference, the maker's result is mostly a
statement about who was on the other side. Adverse selection after the fill is close to zero in vol points
for every class; in USDC it is the underlying's next move, which a hedge removes. The difference between a good
and a bad counterparty shows up at the fill, in the half spread. The loss is carried
by ten wallets, and the observables that the literature uses in place of identity do not find them.
% src const:top_wallets ten

Three of four pre-registered hypotheses are rejected, and the rejections are informative rather than empty.
Toxicity is concentrated but not identifiable from trade size, and from sweeps only through the move of the
underlying; under the registered rule, a
net edge survives in 71.7 per cent of the cells and in short-dated at-the-money options, where H4 expected it to vanish; and an event
study around the listing cannot separate the listing from a decline that began before it. What remains is a
measurement, a fact about identity, and a map that says where on this surface a maker is paid, and how
thinly.
